# Fusing charge and between-charge measurements for health estimation on unseen battery kinds

Chronos-2, whether frozen, fine-tuned or used as an in-context reader, did not beat guessing the average health on battery kinds held out from training. Reformulating the task around physics-normalised capacity ratios, calibrated in context by K labelled examples, and fusing each charge with the measurements a cell collects between charges, lowers the error on unseen kinds from 2.46 points of health (single-charge ratio) to 1.40 points at K = 20.

The proposal asked whether a frozen network can estimate the health of an unseen battery kind from a 30-minute clip of driving current and voltage, given K labelled clips of the same kind in context. I tested this with Chronos-2 (120M parameters) as the encoder: frozen embeddings with a ridge head, LoRA at ranks 8, 32 and 128, Chronos-2's own forecast head used as the in-context reader, and a transformer reader trained on disguised episodes so that it had to use its examples. Under leave-one-kind-out over the five development kinds (KIT, XJTU, CMU, Stanford LG, Stanford NCA), none of these beat the mean label of the K examples; the in-context reader scored a skill of −0.25. A diagnostic explains why: frozen embeddings explain almost none of the health variation within a kind even for kinds seen in training (skill 0.07), and hand-built clip features improved on the average only by 8–11%. A 30-minute driving clip carries too little health information for any learner to transfer, so I changed what the model is given rather than the model.

During a steady charge, the charge needed to move the voltage across a fixed window is proportional to capacity. Dividing it by the same quantity from the cell's own earliest charges gives a label-free ratio that tracks ageing within a cell almost linearly, and differs between kinds mainly in scale and offset. Fitting those two numbers by least squares on the K examples gave 1.97 points mean error on the unseen kinds, against 4.66 for the average guess. This is, however, the setting of existing work: Wang et al. (2024) report 0.87 points and Che et al. (2025) 1.35 on kinds seen in training, and Feng et al. (2024) report about 1.4 points on three new kinds and about 8 on a fourth after test-time training. The closest study to real use, Park et al. (2026, 1,114 electric vehicles), applies the same charge measurement to fleet data and shows both that it works and where it falls short. A single charge reading varies by 5–7% within one car, so the authors trust it only at fleet level. Seasonal temperature dominated month-to-month readings. Only 65–93% of cars had a qualifying slow charge in a year, and fast charges are excluded. A new cell design (VW MEB) needed its own calibration. I also checked whether usable charges are rare in our data; they are not, as nearly every cell has steady charges, so a lack of charges is not the gap.

What these methods share is that each estimate rests on one charge, compared against a fixed reference: the fleet's 90th percentile in Park et al., the cell's earliest charges in my version. Between two charges, a cell keeps producing information that this discards. Each time it rests, its resting voltage fixes its state of charge, so the charge counted between two rests, compared with what a new cell of the same kind needs between the same resting voltages, is an independent capacity measurement taken during driving. The conditions of each charge (current, voltage window, temperature relative to the reference) explain part of its error. Counting charge between rests is a known technique on its own; the contribution here is to combine it with the charge readings and to calibrate the combination for a new kind from a few examples.

I therefore imitate a car that estimates its own health at every steady charge, using only what it has recorded up to that moment and none of its own labels. Each estimate is a vector of 15 label-free quantities: the charge ratio and the median and spread of the last five, the charge conditions, the latest full charge from rest, the capacity ratios of the most recent parking-stop discharges and short charges relative to the cell's early life, and the variability and length of recent drives. The K labelled charges from the new kind's example cells fit a ridge regression on this vector, with the penalty chosen by leaving one example cell out. The learner is still in context in the sense of Garg et al. (2022): a linear map fitted from the examples at test time, with no retraining. What changes is the input, from one clip to a cell's recent measurement history. An MLP embedding meta-trained on the other kinds was also tested; with only four training kinds per fold it does worse than ridge (1.66 against 1.45 at K = 20).

All methods are scored on the same moments, every steady charge of the scored cells, at K = 20, averaged over the five kinds. Park et al.'s protocol is re-implemented from their Methods: the constant-current segment of each charge, current CV below 25%, at least 5 minutes after trimming 3 minutes at each end, temperature 10–35 °C, the charge in a fixed 0.1 V window, normalised by the kind's 90th percentile. Read directly as health, it gives 6.79 points; calibrated with the same K-example line as every other method, 3.62. Its normalisation compares cells with each other, and our lab kinds mix cells tested under different conditions, so this setting is less favourable to it than a fleet of identical cars. Referencing each cell to its own early charges is the largest single step (2.46). Adding information in groups then lowers the error to 1.40, and every group contributes. A handful of recent measurements suffices: one parking-stop pair already gives 1.44, three past charges 1.42, and very old measurements (100 pairs) raise the error to 1.50. Trip length alone scores at the floor, and removing it leaves the result unchanged, so the gain does not come from a fixed laboratory drive lasting shorter as the cell ages. Per kind, the single-charge ratio and the fused vector give KIT 3.18 and 2.09, XJTU 0.79 and 0.96, CMU 4.05 and 1.12, Stanford LG 2.19 and 1.46, and Stanford NCA 2.07 and 1.39. The main limitations are that laboratory chambers hold temperature constant, so the seasonal effect reported by Park et al. is not tested; that laboratory charges are cleaner and more frequent than real ones; that Feng et al.'s method is cited but not reproduced; and that the NASA test kind remains untouched.
